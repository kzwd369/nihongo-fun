## The Ritual of the Launch Sequence

Robot anime lives and dies by its launch sequences. Before any battle, before any dramatic confrontation, the mecha must activate, the pilot must board, and the machine must leave the hangar. This ritual — repeated almost identically each episode — is built on a precise military-technical vocabulary. The words are clipped, commanding, and designed to sound like they belong in an operational cockpit. Learning them means learning the language of mecha anime's most iconic moments.

## Shutsugeki — "Sortie"

> **Shutsugeki (出撃)** — sortie, deployment, scramble

*Shutsu* (出) means "go out"; *geki* (撃) means "strike" or "attack." Together: going out to fight. *Shutsugeki* is the formal military order to deploy — it appears on mission briefing screens, is barked by commanding officers, and is the word that starts every mecha battle. In *Mobile Suit Gundam*, mission control issues the *shutsugeki* command and the pilot responds by moving to the catapult.

The word carries weight because it implies that the pilot is leaving a safe zone and entering combat. There is no casual version. *Shutsugeki* is always serious.

Related deployment vocabulary:
- *Shutsugeki junbi* (出撃準備) — "prepare for sortie"
- *Shutsugeki kaishi* (出撃開始) — "commence sortie"
- *Shutsugeki meirei* (出撃命令) — "sortie order"
- *Kinkyuu shutsugeki* (緊急出撃) — "emergency scramble"

## Hasshin — "Launch"

> **Hasshin (発進)** — launch, departure

If *shutsugeki* is the order, *hasshin* is the physical act of launching. The mecha is on the catapult, the bay doors are open, and the pilot calls *hasshin!* as they accelerate out of the ship. *Gundam*'s most famous launch line is Amuro Ray's *Amuro, ikimasu!* ("Amuro, launching!"), but the technical word for what he is doing is *hasshin*.

In *Macross*, Valkyrie pilots *hasshin* from carrier decks. In *Evangelion*, the Evas are *hasshin* from underground launch bays using massive rail systems. The setting changes, but the word stays.

Launch sequence vocabulary:
- *Hasshin shite kudasai* (発進してください) — "Please launch" (polite command)
- *Hasshin junbi kanryou* (発進準備完了) — "Launch preparations complete"
- *Catapult hasshin* (カタパルト発進) — "Catapult launch"

## Kidou — "Activation"

> **Kidou (起動)** — activation, boot-up, startup

Before a mecha can *hasshin*, it needs to *kidou* — power on. *Ki* (起) means "rise" or "start"; *dou* (動) means "move." The activation sequence is its own mini-ritual: lights flicker on inside the cockpit, displays illuminate, and the operating system initializes. In *Evangelion*, the Eva activation process is deliberately unsettling — the entry plug fills with LCL fluid, neural connections sync, and the pilot waits to see if the machine will accept them.

*Kidou* also appears in contexts beyond mecha:
- *Kidou suru* (起動する) — "to boot up / activate"
- *Sai-kidou* (再起動) — "reboot / restart"
- *Kidou jikan* (起動時間) — "startup time"

The word has a technical, computer-science feel that fits the genre's fusion of military and engineering aesthetics.

## Shisutemu Ooru Guriin — "System All Green"

> **Shisutemu ooru guriin (システムオールグリーン)** — "all systems green / nominal"

This phrase is Japanese-English — *shisutemu* from "system," *ooru* from "all," *guriin* from "green." Mecha anime borrows heavily from English-language military and aviation terminology, filtering it through Japanese pronunciation. The bridge crew confirms *ooru guriin* to signal that every subsystem is operational and the mecha is ready for combat.

Other cockpit status phrases:
- *Enerugi juuten kanryou* (エネルギー充填完了) — "Energy charge complete"
- *Haichi ni tsuku* (配置につく) — "Taking battle positions"
- *Sentou kakuritsu* (戦闘確率) — used in probability-of-survival readouts
- *Mokuhyou hosoku* (目標捕捉) — "Target acquired"
- *Shougai nashi* (障害なし) — "No obstacles / all clear"

## Gattai — "Combine" (The Mecha Context)

> **Gattai (合体)** — combine, merge

The article on [transformations](/en/anime-japanese/transformations/) covers *gattai* as a transformation call. In the mecha context, *gattai* is specifically an operational command — multiple units physically docking and reassembling into a larger robot. The process is mechanical, not magical. Bolts lock, panels rotate, and a new cockpit configuration activates.

In *Getter Robo*, three jets combine into different robot forms depending on the order of docking. In *Voltron* (Japanese: *GoLion*), five lion-shaped mechs merge into a humanoid robot. The *gattai* sequence is the centerpiece of these shows — repeated every episode, merchandise built around it, and audiences cheering for the moment the combined form stands complete.

Mecha-specific *gattai* terms:
- *Gattai shiro!* (合体しろ！) — "Combine!" (command form)
- *Gattai kaijo* (合体解除) — "Separation / uncombine"
- *Saishuu gattai* (最終合体) — "Final combination"

## The Cockpit Vocabulary

Inside the cockpit, mecha pilots and their support crews use a distinct register — half military jargon, half engineering shorthand:

- *Hasshin kauntodaun* (発進カウントダウン) — launch countdown
- *Teki-ki sekkin* (敵機接近) — "Enemy unit approaching"
- *Buki sentaku* (武器選択) — "Weapon select"
- *Soujuu kankaku seibi* (操縦感覚整備) — "Control sensitivity calibrated"
- *Tsuushin kairo hiraku* (通信回路開く) — "Open communication channel"

This vocabulary creates the *texture* of mecha anime — the feeling that the robot is a real machine operated by trained personnel, not a magical suit that responds to willpower alone.

## Real-World Roots

Mecha anime vocabulary draws from actual Japanese military and aviation terminology. *Shutsugeki* is used in Japan's Self-Defense Forces. *Hasshin* appears in space program communications. The genre's founders — Yoshiyuki Tomino (*Gundam*), Go Nagai (*Mazinger Z*) — deliberately borrowed from military language to ground their fantastical robots in operational realism. This is part of why mecha anime has a different *feel* from magical girl or shonen transformation scenes: the vocabulary comes from manuals, not mythology.

## The Great Mecha Series and Their Signature Calls

Different franchises have developed their own launch traditions:

- **Gundam**: *"(Pilot name), (unit name), shutsugeki suru!"* — formal, military
- **Evangelion**: *"Eva shogo-ki, hasshin!"* — clinical, institutional
- **Macross**: *"(Callsign), hasshin!"* — aviation-style
- **Mazinger Z**: *"Pilder on!"* — docking the pilot vehicle onto the robot
- **Gurren Lagann**: *"Ore o dare da to omotte yagaru!"* — "Who the hell do you think I am!" (breaks every protocol, which is the point)

*Gurren Lagann* is notable for replacing standard mecha procedure with raw emotion — a deliberate subversion that only works because the audience knows what the conventions are.

## Fun Fact

*Space Battleship Yamato* (1974) popularized the phrase *enerugi juuten 120%* ("energy charge 120%") during its iconic Wave Motion Gun firing sequence. The countdown — delivered calmly by bridge crew while the screen fills with building energy — became so famous that it influenced how real Japanese TV news programs frame dramatic countdowns. The vocabulary of anime mecha has leaked into broader Japanese pop culture in ways that most viewers no longer notice.
